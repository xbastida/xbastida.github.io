Dbizi · San Sebastián

Redesigning the bike-sharing network

A data-driven model that finds where to place stations so more people can ride, fewer staff hours are spent rebalancing bikes, and fewer truck trips pollute the city.

The problem

Dbizi has 69 stations across the city. Many were placed years ago by intuition. Some are chronically empty at rush hour; others sit full all day and require constant rebalancing. With a full year of real trip data now available, we can do better.

This tool asks: given a budget of N stations, where should they go to maximise ridership while minimising operational burden — without leaving whole neighbourhoods out of walking range?

Three things the model cares about

🚲
Demand

How much cycling activity a location is expected to attract. A gradient-boosting model (LightGBM) is trained on the 69 current stations — on trips per m² of catchment area, not raw trip counts — and then applied citywide to hexagonal cells of ~2 200 m². Each station captures demand from its surroundings, shaped by elevation, nearby cycling infrastructure, residential population, and the concentration of points of interest such as workplaces, restaurants, and transit stops. Leave-one-station-out R² = 0.66.

⚖️
Operating cost

Some locations structurally generate imbalance: gravity, commute patterns, and uneven land use create persistent flows that staff must reverse by van every day. Cost is built from three pillars — LIS (Lost Imbalance Surplus: bike-hours outside a ±10 bike comfort band), inflated by day-to-day unpredictability and by the drive time from the nearest main road corridor, so erratic and hard-to-reach stations pay extra.

👥
Population coverage

Some parts of the city will never be high-demand corridors — and some are an operational headache to serve. But they still need access. Coverage enters as a hard constraint, not a penalty: the layout must put at least a chosen percentage of residents within 200 m of a station, whatever the demand surface says about those neighbourhoods.

What predicts demand?

The model was trained on decensored Dbizi trip counts per station, normalised by an overlap-corrected catchment area. Nine neighbourhood-level features describe every hexagon:

Bike lane length nearby
Workers in the area
Restaurants, bars & shops within 300 m
Residential population (age-weighted)
Schools & universities nearby
Land-use entropy (mix of uses)
Altitude (steeper = less use)
City-centre flag
Income per person

How the optimizer works

1
Score every candidate site

The city is filtered down to ~5 000 buildable hexagons — at least 500 residents within 300 m, no river, no pedestrianised Old Town. Each one carries a demand score and an operating-cost score, both normalised to [0, 1].

2
Write it as an integer program

One yes/no variable per hexagon. Maximise W × demand − (1 − W) × cost, subject to four rules: exactly N stations; 26 stations with electrical hookups or at intermodal nodes are kept; the coverage floor is met; and no two stations sit closer than their minimum separation.

3
Solve to proven optimality

An open-source solver (CBC, via PuLP) searches the layout space, discarding whole families of layouts it can prove will not beat the best found so far. Unlike a heuristic, it certifies the answer: no other layout satisfying the same rules scores higher. That separation distance adapts — it shrinks as the network densifies, and busy hexagons are allowed closer neighbours than quiet ones.

4
Size each station

The total dock budget scales with the station count — 1 232 docks at N = 70, the current network's total. Within that budget, stations with high demand and high rebalancing burden get more docks; every station stays between 15 and 25.

What you can tune in the demo

Every combination below was solved in advance; the physical panel simply calls up the matching layout on screen. Extra toggles reveal the demand score field, a day of simulated trips, and how much population and workplaces fall within 100 / 200 / 300 m of a station.

N
Number of stations
How many stations to place, from 70 to 120. The current network has 69. More stations improve coverage and unlock latent demand, but increase capital cost.
W
Demand weight
W = 1 → pure ridership maximisation. Lower W shifts weight onto operating cost, hunting for cheaper-to-run locations. Reference value 0.6.
COV
Coverage target
The minimum share of residents that must live within 200 m of a station, from 50 % to 100 %. Push it high enough and no layout can satisfy both coverage and spacing — the problem becomes infeasible.
M
Mandatory stations
On, the 26 stations with an expensive grid connection or an intermodal role are locked in place. Off, the optimizer redraws the network from scratch — a useful "what if we started over?" comparison.
