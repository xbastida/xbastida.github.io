Redesigning the bike-sharing network

A data-driven model that finds where to place stations so more people can ride and fewer truck trips pollute the city.

The problem

The method described here works for any docked bike-sharing system. The case study is Dbizi, the bike-sharing system of San Sebastián, which has run since 2020 with 70 stations across the city, most of them sited by intuition. Some are chronically empty at rush hour; others sit full all day and require constant rebalancing. Years of recorded trips are now available, over a million in 2024 alone, and a full system redesign is scheduled for 2028.

This tool asks: given a budget of N stations, where should they go to maximise ridership while minimising operational cost, without leaving whole neighbourhoods out of walking range?

The grid

The city is discretised into H3 hexagons. Every candidate location is one hexagon, and each carries the two scores below.

Three things the model cares about

🚲
Demand

How much cycling activity a location is expected to attract, measured as arrivals plus departures per year divided by the station's effective catchment area. Two predictors are combined. LightGBM learns the relationship between that usage density and the urban variables surrounding each of the 70 current stations, listed further down, and extends it to every hexagon in the city. A geographic KNN instead interpolates the observed performance of the nearest current stations. The two are blended, with the weight shifting toward the interpolation as a hexagon lies closer to an existing station and toward the model as it lies further away. Leave-one-station-out R² = 0.66.

⚖️
Operational cost

Some locations structurally generate imbalance: topography, commute patterns, and uneven land use produce persistent net flows that operators must reverse by van. Cost = LIS × (1 + α × instability + β × road distance). LIS (Lost Imbalance Surplus) measures how much rebalancing work a station would generate; instability measures how predictable that work is, and therefore how far ahead the rebalancing team can plan for it; road distance measures how long the rebalancing van takes to get there, as the drive time along the street network from the nearest principal corridor. Rebalancing need and instability are predicted citywide by the same LightGBM-plus-KNN procedure used for demand; road distance is calculated directly from the road network.

👥
Population coverage

Some parts of the city will never be high-demand corridors, and some are operationally costly to serve. But they still need access. A minimum coverage requirement is therefore imposed on the layout: at least a chosen percentage of residents must live within 200 m of a station, whatever the demand and cost surfaces say about those neighbourhoods.

How the optimizer works

1
Filter the hexagons and score them

The grid is filtered down to ~5 000 buildable hexagons: at least 500 residents within 300 m, no river, no pedestrianised Old Town. Each one carries a demand score and an operating-cost score, calculated by the procedure explained above and both normalised to [0, 1].

2
Write it as an integer program

One yes/no variable per hexagon. Maximise W × demand − (1 − W) × cost, subject to four rules: exactly N stations; the 26 stations with electrical hookups or at intermodal nodes are kept, if that option is active; the coverage floor is met; and no two stations sit closer than their exclusion radius.

3
Solve to proven optimality

An open-source solver (CBC, via PuLP) searches the layout space, discarding whole families of layouts it can prove will not beat the best found so far. Unlike a heuristic, it certifies the answer: no other layout satisfying the same rules scores higher. The minimum separation is set by a variable exclusion radius drawn around each station: it shrinks as the network densifies, and busy locations carry a smaller radius than quiet ones, so they may sit closer together.

4
Size each station

The total dock budget scales with the station count: 1 232 docks at N = 70, the current network's total. Within that budget, stations with high demand and high rebalancing burden get more docks; every station stays between 15 and 25.

What you can tune in the demo

Every combination below was solved in advance; the physical panel simply calls up the matching layout on screen, alongside the demand score field and a day of simulated trips.

N
Number of stations
How many stations to place, from 70 to 120. The current network has 70. More stations improve coverage and unlock latent demand, but increase capital cost.
W
Demand weight
W = 1 → pure ridership maximisation. Lower W shifts weight onto operating cost, favouring cheaper-to-run locations. Reference value 0.6.
W × demand − (1 − W) × cost
COV
Coverage target
The minimum share of residents that must live within 200 m of a station, from 50 % to 95 %. Some combinations have no solution at all: 95 % of the population cannot be reached with only 70 stations, for instance.
M
Mandatory stations
On, the 26 stations with an expensive grid connection or an intermodal role are locked in place. Off, the optimizer redraws the network from scratch, a useful "what if we started over?" comparison.
